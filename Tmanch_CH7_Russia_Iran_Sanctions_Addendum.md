# Chapter 7 Addendum — Russia, Iran, and the 2026 Russian-Oil Relief Contradiction
## Strategic cooperation, reported targeting intelligence, sanctions licenses, and attribution
### Coverage: January 2025–August 26, 2026

## Executive finding

The 2026 Russia–Iran record materially reduces the exculpatory value of Trump-administration sanctions against Russia.

The public evidence shows:

1. Russia and Iran deepened strategic, security, intelligence, military, energy, and sanctions-resistance cooperation.
2. U.S. officials reportedly assessed that Russia supplied Iran information concerning American military targets during the 2026 conflict.
3. Trump publicly acknowledged that Russia might be helping Iran and analogized that assistance to U.S. support for Ukraine.
4. The Trump administration nevertheless issued a succession of general licenses authorizing delivery and sale of specified Russian-origin oil cargoes during the resulting energy shock.
5. The licenses were temporary and limited; they did not remove the entire Russia sanctions program.
6. Their practical effect was to permit Russian oil to reach market and generate revenue at a time when Russia was credibly reported to be assisting a state attacking U.S. forces.

The most defensible conclusion is therefore:

> **When Russian pressure conflicted with the administration’s short-term energy-price and market-stability objectives, the administration accepted a measurable Russian economic benefit even amid reported Russian assistance to Iran. This weakens the claim that sanctions announcements demonstrate a durable Trump hard line toward Moscow.**

---

# 1. Russia–Iran strategic partnership

On January 17, 2025, Vladimir Putin and Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian signed a comprehensive strategic partnership treaty.

The agreement covered or contemplated:

- trade and investment;
- energy;
- military and security cooperation;
- intelligence coordination;
- science and technology;
- sanctions resistance;
- and broader political alignment against Western pressure.

The treaty did not create a NATO-style automatic mutual-defense obligation. It did formalize a long-term framework for cooperation between two heavily sanctioned governments.

Source:

- [Associated Press — Russia and Iran sign strategic partnership treaty](https://apnews.com/article/71a20990373851741d1fe76a81699036)

## Evidentiary use

The treaty establishes a formal strategic relationship and makes later intelligence or military assistance more plausible.

## Limit

The treaty alone does not prove that Russia supplied a particular target package, weapon, or operational order.

---

# 2. Reported Russian assistance to Iran against U.S. forces

On March 6, 2026, *The Washington Post* reported that Russia was supplying Iran targeting information concerning American warships and aircraft in the Middle East.

The report cited three officials familiar with the intelligence and described the assistance as including locations of U.S. military assets.

Source:

- [Washington Post — Russia is providing Iran intelligence to target U.S. forces](https://www.washingtonpost.com/national-security/2026/03/06/russia-iran-intelligence-us-targets/)

## Evidence classification

**Strongly sourced intelligence reporting; not a public judicial or declassified intelligence finding.**

## What it supports

- U.S. officials had intelligence indicating Russian operational assistance to Iran.
- The reported assistance concerned American military targets.
- Russia’s relationship with Iran had direct U.S. national-security implications.

## What it does not prove by itself

- the complete scope of Russian assistance;
- whether every Iranian strike used Russian information;
- Putin’s personal order;
- or the full reliability of the classified assessment.

---

# 3. Trump’s public acknowledgment

On March 13, 2026, Trump said Russia “might be” helping Iran and suggested Putin could view that assistance as analogous to U.S. support for Ukraine.

Source:

- [Forbes — Trump says Russia might be helping Iran](https://www.forbes.com/sites/saradorn/2026/03/13/trump-says-russia-might-be-helping-iran-contradicting-top-advisor/)

## Analytical significance

Trump did not publicly treat possible Russian assistance to Iran as an intolerable betrayal requiring immediate punitive escalation. He normalized it as reciprocal great-power behavior.

That response is relevant because it reveals the president’s own framing:

> Russia helping a state attacking U.S. forces could be understood as an offset to American support for Ukraine.

This framing may explain policy restraint without proving improper influence. It also weakens the proposition that later sanctions relief occurred because the administration believed Russia was acting as a reliable U.S. partner.

---

# 4. General License 133 — Russian oil to India

On March 5, 2026, OFAC issued General License 133, authorizing delivery and sale to India of Russian-origin crude oil and petroleum products loaded on vessels as of that date.

Source:

- [OFAC — General License 133](https://ofac.treasury.gov/recent-actions/20260305_33)

The license later expired on April 4, 2026 according to OFAC’s archive.

## Effect

The license allowed specified Russian oil already in transit to be completed and sold rather than stranded or blocked.

## Limit

It did not authorize unlimited future Russian production or permanently terminate the Russia sanctions regime.

---

# 5. General Licenses 134, 134A, 134B, and 134C

OFAC subsequently issued and amended a broader series of authorizations concerning Russian-origin crude oil and petroleum products loaded by specified dates.

Sources:

- [OFAC — General License 134, March 12, 2026](https://ofac.treasury.gov/recent-actions/20260312_33)
- [OFAC — General License 134A, March 19, 2026](https://ofac.treasury.gov/recent-actions/20260319_33)
- [OFAC — General License 134B, April 17, 2026](https://ofac.treasury.gov/recent-actions/20260417_33)
- [OFAC archive of expired and superseded licenses](https://ofac.treasury.gov/sanctions-programs-and-country-information/venezuela-related-sanctions/archive-of-selected-revoked-and-expired-general-licenses)

The archive records that:

- GL 134 was superseded by GL 134A;
- GL 134A expired April 11, 2026;
- GL 134B expired May 16, 2026;
- GL 134C expired June 17, 2026.

## Effect

The series provided time-limited authorization for already-loaded Russian petroleum cargoes to be delivered and sold.

## Practical beneficiary

- purchasers and shippers avoided stranded cargoes;
- global supply received additional barrels;
- energy-price pressure was reduced;
- and Russian sellers received or preserved transaction value.

## Conventional explanation

The Iran conflict and disruption of energy markets created a global supply shock. The administration could reasonably seek to prevent severe price spikes, shortages, or allied economic damage.

## Counterintelligence significance

The conventional explanation does not convert the relief into anti-Russian policy. It establishes that Russia pressure was subordinate to another Trump priority.

# 6. Rosneft and Lukoil sanctions must be described with implementation detail

Treasury designated Rosneft and Lukoil on October 22, 2025.

Source:

- [Treasury — sanctions on major Russian oil companies](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/sb0290)

The designations were meaningful. They were accompanied and followed by licenses involving:

- wind-down transactions;
- debt, equity, and derivatives;
- non-Russian Lukoil retail stations;
- Bulgarian operations;
- Caspian Pipeline and Central Asian projects;
- and repeated authorization to negotiate a sale of Lukoil International GmbH while maintaining specified operations.

Relevant sources include:

- [OFAC October 22, 2025 designations and licenses](https://ofac.treasury.gov/recent-actions/20251022)
- [OFAC November 14, 2025 licenses](https://ofac.treasury.gov/recent-actions/20251114)
- [OFAC April 14, 2026 amended licenses](https://ofac.treasury.gov/recent-actions/20260414)
- [OFAC August 20, 2026 General License 131I](https://ofac.treasury.gov/recent-actions/20260820)

## Correct formulation

It is inaccurate to say the Rosneft and Lukoil designations were wholly fictitious or simply erased.

It is also inaccurate to cite the headline designations without disclosing the extensive licenses, market protections, and later Russian-oil authorizations.

The defensible language is:

> **The Trump administration imposed significant designations against Rosneft and Lukoil, then repeatedly managed, narrowed, and partially offset their practical impact through wind-down, retail, divestment, third-country, and energy-market licenses.**

# 7. India tariff and later removal

Trump imposed an additional 25-percent tariff on Indian imports because of India’s Russian-oil purchases.

The tariff was removed effective February 7, 2026 after India represented that it would stop directly or indirectly importing Russian oil and expand U.S. energy purchases and defense cooperation.

Sources:

- [White House — removal of the Russia-oil-related India tariff](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2026/02/modifying-duties-to-address-threats-to-the-united-states-by-the-government-of-the-russian-federation-04b2/)
- [White House — U.S.–India trade fact sheet](https://www.whitehouse.gov/fact-sheets/2026/02/fact-sheet-the-united-states-and-india-announce-historic-trade-deal/)

## Correct formulation

The tariff was a real but temporary pressure measure. Its success depends on evidence that Indian direct and indirect Russian-oil purchases actually stopped rather than shifted through intermediaries or changed documentation.

Its removal and the subsequent Russian-oil cargo licenses make it weak evidence of sustained strategic opposition to Moscow.

# 8. Safeguard removal is direct affirmative evidence

The Russia–Iran contradiction must be considered alongside the administration’s direct institutional choices.

On February 5, 2025, Attorney General Pamela Bondi issued memoranda that:

- disbanded Task Force KleptoCapture;
- ended the Kleptocracy Team;
- ended the Kleptocracy Asset Recovery Initiative;
- disbanded the Foreign Influence Task Force;
- narrowed criminal FARA and Section 951 policy toward traditional-espionage-like conduct;
- and disbanded the National Security Division Corporate Enforcement Unit.

Source:

- [Department of Justice — Attorney General select publications and February 5 memoranda](https://www.justice.gov/ag/select-publications)
- [DOJ charging memorandum](https://www.justice.gov/ag/media/1388541/dl?inline=)

Other administration actions closed or reduced dedicated capacity involving:

- foreign information manipulation;
- election-security information sharing;
- and foreign-malign-influence coordination.

These actions cannot be attributed to NATO allies, Congress, or institutional resistance. They were administration policy choices and therefore carry greater weight in a Trump-centered influence assessment than allied-funded weapons packages carry as counterevidence.

---

# 9. Net assessment

The 2026 record does not support the statement that Trump never took any action harmful to Russia.

It supports a more troubling and precise conclusion:

1. Trump-administration pressure on Russia was intermittent and highly transactional.
2. Some adverse measures were real but late, reactive, limited, or implemented after institutional and allied pressure.
3. Several prominent threats were never implemented as announced.
4. Some implemented measures were later reversed or diluted.
5. Allies and institutions supplied much of the strongest continuing support for Ukraine.
6. Direct administration choices weakened safeguards against Russian illicit wealth and influence.
7. The administration relaxed defined Russian-oil restrictions even when Russia was credibly reported to be assisting Iran against U.S. forces.

The proper conclusion is not uninterrupted Russian command.

It is:

> **A pattern of susceptibility and accommodation in which Trump repeatedly prioritized personal diplomacy, market stability, burden transfer, political advantage, or commercial transaction over sustained strategic pressure on Moscow—even when Russia’s conduct directly threatened American forces and interests.**
