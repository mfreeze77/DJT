# 1. Executive finding

Cambridge Analytica is relevant to the Manafort–Kilimnik inquiry because it demonstrates that the Trump campaign operated inside a sophisticated domestic data, voter-segmentation, survey, Facebook-advertising, and message-testing environment at the same time sensitive internal campaign information reached Russian intelligence.

It is **not presently proved** that Cambridge Analytica was the operational bridge between:

```text
Manafort / Gates campaign information
                ↓
            Kilimnik
                ↓
      Russian intelligence
                ↓
 IRA Facebook targeting, GRU actions,
 WikiLeaks release planning, or another operation
```

The strongest defensible conclusion is:

> **Cambridge Analytica shows that the campaign possessed machinery capable of converting voter information into targeted digital communications. Treasury establishes that sensitive campaign polling and strategy reached Russian intelligence. Russia was simultaneously conducting social-media, hacking, publication, and influence operations. The missing evidence is the routing record showing whether, where, and how those systems intersected.**

That missing bridge prevents a finding that one named Manafort file selected one named Facebook audience, district, advertisement, or release date. It does not make Russian intelligence receipt or likely exploitation of the information insignificant.

# 3. The four overlapping systems

## 3.1 Trump campaign internal information

The campaign possessed private information capable of revealing:

- battleground-state assessments;
- private polling that could differ from public surveys;
- geographic and demographic vulnerabilities;
- turnout assumptions;
- persuadable groups;
- message-testing results;
- campaign resource allocation;
- and leadership expectations concerning the path to victory.

Manafort and Rick Gates shared internal information with Konstantin Kilimnik. Treasury later identified Kilimnik as a known Russian Intelligence Services agent and stated that he provided Russian intelligence with sensitive polling and campaign-strategy information.

**Status:** official receipt finding.

**Principal source:** [Treasury, April 15, 2021](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/jy0126)

## 3.2 Cambridge Analytica and domestic voter targeting

Cambridge Analytica worked within the Trump campaign’s data and digital environment and offered voter profiling, audience segmentation, surveys, message testing, and targeted-advertising services.

The Federal Trade Commission found that Cambridge Analytica used deceptively obtained Facebook information to generate personality scores, matched those scores with U.S. voter records, and used the resulting products for voter profiling and targeted-advertising services.

The FTC record establishes that the Kogan application collected information from approximately 250,000–270,000 U.S. app users and tens of millions of Facebook friends, including at least 30 million identifiable U.S. consumers.

**Status:** official FTC finding concerning Cambridge Analytica’s data collection and voter-profiling system.

**Principal sources:**

- [FTC case and final order against Cambridge Analytica](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/cases-proceedings/182-3107-cambridge-analytica-llc-matter)
- [FTC Kogan/Nix case record](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/cases-proceedings/182-3106-182-3107-aleksandr-kogan-alexander-nix-matter)
- [FTC explanation of voter profiling and targeted advertising](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2019/07/ftc-sues-cambridge-analytica-settles-former-ceo-app-developer)

## 3.3 Internet Research Agency social-media operation

The IRA used false American identities, social-media personas, political advertisements, rallies, divisive content, and American infrastructure to influence U.S. political discourse and the 2016 election.

**Status:** federal indictment and intelligence attribution; not a merits conviction of every foreign defendant.

**Principal source:** [DOJ Internet Research Agency indictment announcement](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/grand-jury-indicts-thirteen-russian-individuals-and-three-russian-companies-scheme-interfere)

## 3.4 GRU hacking and publication system

Russian military-intelligence officers hacked Democratic and Clinton-associated targets, stole material, operated release personas and websites, and staged publication through online channels.

**Status:** federal indictment and intelligence attribution; no public finding that the campaign participated in the hacking.

**Principal source:** [DOJ GRU indictment announcement](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/grand-jury-indicts-12-russian-intelligence-officers-hacking-offenses-related-2016-election)

# 4. What the disputed sentence means

The prior row stated:

> **A particular file directly selected a Facebook audience, voting district, advertisement, or GRU/WikiLeaks release date — plausible but not publicly established.**

A more precise formulation is:

> **A named Manafort/Gates campaign file was directly used to define a particular Facebook custom audience, geographic or demographic voter segment, IRA message or advertisement, GRU action, or WikiLeaks publication decision — plausible and testable, but not established in the public record.**

The phrase does **not** mean that Russian use of the data is unlikely. It means the public record does not yet contain a complete trace such as:

1. the native campaign file and metadata;
2. proof that Kilimnik transmitted that exact file or a unique derivative;
3. a Russian intelligence ingestion or analytical record;
4. dissemination to a named influence component;
5. matching audience definitions, voter fields, locations, or message categories;
6. operator communications attributing the decision to the campaign-derived information; and
7. a resulting advertisement, audience, release, or operational action.

“Voting district” should be read broadly. Digital targeting could use:

- state, county, metropolitan area, or ZIP-code geography;
- age and demographic categories;
- interests and behavior;
- engagement patterns;
- email or phone custom audiences;
- lookalike audiences;
- issue-based segments;
- turnout or persuasion models;
- or combinations of commercial and political data.

# 6. Cambridge Analytica and WikiLeaks

## 6.1 Nix’s admitted approach

In February 2018, Alexander Nix first told the UK Parliament that Cambridge Analytica had no relationship with WikiLeaks. Later in the same hearing, when confronted with Julian Assange’s public account, Nix acknowledged that Cambridge Analytica had used an intermediary to ask whether Assange would meet to discuss information reportedly relevant to the election. Nix said the approach was rejected.

In June 2018, Nix again described the approach as an effort to learn what material Assange possessed and acknowledged that the motivation was connected to information potentially relevant to the Trump campaign.

**What this establishes:**

- Cambridge Analytica leadership sought access to the stolen-material publication environment;
- the approach occurred during the election;
- and the approach was rejected.

**What it does not establish:**

- a working Cambridge Analytica–WikiLeaks relationship;
- possession of the stolen material;
- coordination of release timing;
- or Russian intelligence direction.

### Sources

- [UK Parliament, Nix oral evidence, February 27, 2018, Q741–Q844](https://committees.parliament.uk/oralevidence/7660/html/)
- [UK Parliament, Nix oral evidence, June 6, 2018, Q3246–Q3257](https://committees.parliament.uk/oralevidence/8065/html/)

# 7. Russia-related data-security and access risks

## 7.1 Kogan’s Russian research

Christopher Wylie testified and submitted written evidence that Aleksandr Kogan was performing Russian-funded psychological-profiling research at St. Petersburg University while managing or participating in the Facebook-data acquisition project.

Wylie expressly stated that he was **not** alleging that Kogan, Nix, Cambridge Analytica, or others knowingly colluded with Russian intelligence. His allegation was that the combination of:

- sensitive Facebook-derived data;
- insecure handling;
- Russian-based work and travel;
- foreign-intelligence interest in psychological profiling;
- and Cambridge Analytica’s military and political information-operations work

created a severe access and counterintelligence risk.

### Sources

- [UK Parliament, Wylie oral evidence, March 27, 2018](https://committees.parliament.uk/oralevidence/7803/html/)
- [Wylie supplementary written evidence](https://committees.parliament.uk/writtenevidence/89475/html/)

## 7.2 Lukoil presentations

Wylie stated that Cambridge Analytica sent or presented materials to senior Lukoil personnel concerning:

- American voter profiling;
- online targeting;
- data capacity;
- rumor campaigns;
- disinformation;
- attitudinal inoculation;
- and confidence in civic institutions.

Nix disputed the sinister interpretation and described the effort as a proposed Turkish petrol-station customer-marketing project that never proceeded.

The record therefore establishes a contested, document-specific inquiry rather than a resolved intelligence transfer.

The decisive evidence is:

- complete presentation decks;
- email recipients and attachments;
- meeting notes;
- data demonstrations;
- follow-up proposals;
- server and download logs;
- and any Russian government or security-service reporting concerning the contact.

### Sources

- [UK Parliament, Wylie oral evidence, March 27, 2018](https://committees.parliament.uk/oralevidence/7803/html/)
- [Wylie supplementary written evidence](https://committees.parliament.uk/writtenevidence/89475/html/)
- [UK Parliament, Nix rebuttal, June 6, 2018](https://committees.parliament.uk/oralevidence/8065/html/)

## 7.3 Russian-associated IP addresses

The House of Commons final report records that the Information Commissioner found IP addresses associated with data or server activity involving Kogan that resolved to Russia and associated states. The report states that some addresses were linked to prior alleged cyberattacks or a Tor entry point, and that the issue was referred to relevant authorities and the National Crime Agency.

This establishes an official investigative referral. It does **not** establish:

- that Russian intelligence accessed the Trump campaign dataset;
- that the IP traffic was malicious;
- that the relevant app was the exact GSR/Trump dataset;
- or that any accessed data reached the IRA or GRU.

### Source

- [House of Commons, *Disinformation and “fake news”: Final Report*, paras. 255–256](https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/cm201719/cmselect/cmcumeds/1791/179109.htm)

# 8. Operational pathway model

A direct file handoff is only one possible pathway.

```text
Manafort / Gates internal information
                 ↓
              Kilimnik
                 ↓
        Russian intelligence intake
                 ↓
     ┌───────────┼────────────┐
     ↓           ↓            ↓
 strategic   counterintelligence   operational
 assessment      assessment        guidance
     ↓           ↓            ↓
 leadership   channel value   sanitized themes,
 briefing     and leverage     priorities, timing,
                              audiences, or resources
```

Cambridge Analytica could relate to this architecture in several different ways:

1. **Parallel capability:** it shows the campaign’s own ability to convert data into targeted communications.
2. **Source vocabulary:** Russian intelligence could use inside polling to understand or validate the campaign’s segmentation logic without receiving Cambridge Analytica’s database.
3. **Vendor-access risk:** campaign vendors, subcontractors, data platforms, and employees could create additional access points.
4. **Security-risk node:** Kogan, Lukoil contacts, insecure data handling, and Russian-associated IP activity created possible foreign-access pathways.
5. **Publication interest:** Nix’s Assange approach shows Cambridge Analytica leadership was interested in potentially election-relevant stolen material.
6. **Actual bridge:** Cambridge Analytica or an associated vendor transmitted data or received guidance from Russian actors.

Only the first five have some public evidentiary support. The sixth remains unproved.

# 9. Competing hypotheses

## Hypothesis A — Russian intelligence used Manafort data for strategic assessment only

**Fit:** High.

The information could help Russian leadership understand:

- the campaign’s path to victory;
- internal confidence;
- potential future policy;
- useful pressure points;
- and Manafort’s continuing value as a channel.

This use would leave no Facebook-ad handoff.

## Hypothesis B — Russian intelligence sanitized the data into operational guidance

**Fit:** Moderate to high.

The raw source could be protected while conclusions were passed to another component as:

- priority states;
- demographic or issue themes;
- turnout vulnerabilities;
- likely release effects;
- or resource-allocation guidance.

Operators might never know the original source.

## Hypothesis C — Cambridge Analytica or an associated vendor formed the missing bridge

**Fit:** Plausible but unestablished.

Supporting facts include:

- campaign targeting work;
- Facebook-derived models;
- Assange outreach;
- Russian-related access risks;
- Lukoil contacts;
- and insecure or distributed technical systems.

Missing proof includes:

- a transmission;
- shared audience files;
- Russian instructions;
- matching unique data fields;
- operator communications;
- or a common technical account.

## Hypothesis D — IRA and GRU relied entirely on independent data

**Fit:** Plausible for many actions.

Russia had:

- public polling;
- commercial data;
- U.S.-based reconnaissance;
- platform analytics;
- audience engagement history;
- and its own research.

This explains operational independence. It does not explain why Russian intelligence received campaign information or make the information valueless.

## Hypothesis E — Russian intelligence received the information and did nothing meaningful with it

**Fit:** Weakest comprehensive explanation.

It requires an active hostile intelligence service, during a priority election operation, to disregard sensitive inside-campaign information with obvious strategic and counterintelligence value.

# 10. Proof thresholds

## Established now

- Cambridge Analytica used Facebook-derived information and voter records for profiling and targeted advertising.
- Cambridge Analytica worked inside the Trump campaign’s data environment.
- Manafort/Gates information reached Kilimnik.
- Kilimnik supplied sensitive polling and strategy to Russian intelligence.
- Russia operated IRA social-media and GRU hacking/release campaigns.
- Cambridge Analytica leadership sought contact with Assange and was rejected.
- UK authorities investigated Russia-related access risks around Kogan-associated data or servers.

## Highly probable inference

- Russian intelligence meaningfully assessed, retained, circulated, or exploited the Manafort/Kilimnik information.

## Plausible but not publicly established

- Cambridge Analytica or an associated vendor served as the bridge to Russian operators.
- Manafort data influenced a named IRA audience or advertisement.
- Manafort data affected a GRU or WikiLeaks release decision.
- Russian actors accessed Cambridge Analytica data through Kogan, Lukoil, Russian IP activity, or another route.

## Not established

- knowing Cambridge Analytica collusion with Russian intelligence;
- Russian intelligence control of Cambridge Analytica;
- direct coordination between Cambridge Analytica and the IRA or GRU;
- or measurable election-result causation.

# 12. Falsification criteria

The Cambridge Analytica bridge hypothesis would be materially weakened by:

- complete vendor and platform records showing no relevant data transfers or shared access;
- proof that Cambridge Analytica had no access to the Manafort/Gates materials;
- proof that the files provided to Kilimnik were stale, public, or operationally useless;
- Russian records showing the information was rejected, quarantined, or never disseminated;
- complete Facebook and IRA records showing no matching unique fields, audiences, or timing;
- and a complete Assange record showing no campaign or publication follow-up beyond the rejected inquiry.

It would be materially strengthened by:

- matching unique fields between campaign files and Russian targeting records;
- common audience hashes or uploaded identifiers;
- Russian analytic products citing internal campaign data;
- Cambridge Analytica/vendor logs showing Russian access or transmission;
- operator testimony;
- instructions linking a campaign-derived insight to a named advertisement or release;
- or communications connecting the Manafort/Kilimnik channel to Cambridge Analytica, the IRA, GRU, or WikiLeaks.

# 13. Conclusion-ready language

> **Cambridge Analytica was part of the Trump campaign’s domestic data-targeting architecture and used Facebook-derived information, voter records, modeling, and targeted advertising. Its leadership also sought contact with Julian Assange, while Russia-related security concerns existed around Aleksandr Kogan’s Russian work, Lukoil presentations, insecure data handling, and Russian-associated IP activity examined by UK authorities. None of those facts publicly proves that Cambridge Analytica transmitted Manafort’s polling to Russia or served as the operational bridge between Kilimnik and the IRA or GRU. They do establish that the campaign possessed a mature data-to-message system and that additional foreign-access pathways warranted investigation. Treasury separately establishes that sensitive campaign information reached Russian intelligence. Meaningful Russian intelligence exploitation is therefore substantially more probable than non-use, while a specific file-to-Facebook, file-to-IRA, file-to-GRU, or file-to-WikiLeaks routing remains one of the most consequential unresolved questions of the 2016 election.**

The concise finding is:

> **Cambridge Analytica proves capability and creates a serious access-risk node. Manafort–Kilimnik proves Russian intelligence receipt. The missing evidence is the operational routing record connecting the two—or showing that the information was exploited through a different Russian channel.**
