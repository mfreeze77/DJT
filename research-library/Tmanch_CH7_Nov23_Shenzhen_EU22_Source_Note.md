# November 23 review: Shenzhen declaration and EU sanctions proposal

## Shenzhen: J20-2026-116

The [White House's November 19 declaration](https://www.whitehouse.gov/briefings-statements/2026/11/u-s-russia-china-heads-of-state-declaration-toward-peace/) and [Chinese Foreign Ministry publication](https://www.mfa.gov.cn/eng/xw/zyxw/202611/t20261119_12063224.html) document that Trump, Putin and Xi met in Shenzhen and endorsed four points. These are two official publications of the same joint instrument, not independent evidence that any measure was implemented.

1. The leaders endorsed UN reform without specifying a text, vote or institutional change.
2. They expressed plans to increase refined petroleum production and supply. The text specifies no volume, deadline, financing, route, offtaker or sanctions change. Compare the distinct U.S. diesel authorization **J20-2026-011**, unresolved cargo implementation **J20-2026-040** and separately limited LIG license **J20-2026-114** in [J20-Pres](J20-Pres.md). Shared subject matter supports examining a broader energy-supply policy pattern, not inferring that Shenzhen authorized that cargo, ordered U.S. imports, or superseded the prior Rosneft/Lukoil designations **J20-040/041**.
3. The leaders emphasized official immunities and state consent and criticized misuse of international-court jurisdiction. The statement neither names a specific case nor alters the [ICC's Ukraine warrants](https://www.icc-cpi.int/situations/ukraine). The [Mongolia noncooperation ruling, ICC-01/22-90](https://www.icc-cpi.int/sites/default/files/CourtRecords/0902ebd1809d1971.pdf) is a concrete comparator: the Chamber rejected official position as a basis not to arrest Putin in that cooperation context. The joint position gives Moscow public diplomatic support for general objections to international-court jurisdiction while Putin is subject to a warrant; it also aligns with the U.S. administration's own previously documented opposition to specified ICC actions against protected persons under [Executive Order 14203](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/02/imposing-sanctions-on-the-international-criminal-court/). Those converging interests are documented. A pledge to protect Putin personally, change to any party's legal duty, or quid pro quo is not.
4. Nuclear risk reduction is a prospective shared benefit. The statement reiterates language present in the [2022 P5 declaration](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/DCPD-202200002/pdf/DCPD-202200002.pdf) and says there will be dialogue. No date, verifiable restriction or arms-control agreement is specified.

**What changes:** confirmed direct presidential engagement, plus joint public political support for expanded refined-product supply and a common stated approach to international-court jurisdiction. Russia gets a joint platform and political support on those points; potential consumer and risk-reduction benefits are shared, not measured. This is not a Ukraine peace deal and it does not prove legal protection, energy receipts or motive. [Zelenskyy's November 23 NATO Parliamentary Assembly address](https://www.president.gov.ua/en/news/zvernennya-prezidenta-do-parlamentskoyi-asambleyi-nato-107449) still describes the next Ukraine-Europe-U.S.-Russia meeting as prospective and the proposed guarantees as lacking specifics. Read alongside **J20-2026-079/080** for earlier negotiations and **J20-2026-115** for November 19 allied coordination; do not collapse these separate forums into one event.

## EU 22nd sanctions package proposal: J20-2026-117

The [European Commission's November 23 statement, STATEMENT/26/2563](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/api/files/document/print/en/statement_26_2563/STATEMENT_26_2563_EN.pdf) describes proposed transaction restrictions targeting around 60 Russian banks, SWIFT exclusion for those banks, coverage of crypto/payment networks including in third countries, wider energy-services limits covering refineries and electricity, more than 70 additional shadow-fleet vessels, and a proposed ban on tankers insured in Russia. Governance, sports/culture, sponsorship and visa measures are also described. The Commission reports a Crypto Sanctions Task Force launch; that is separate from operative financial restrictions. The counts are proposal scope, not effective listing counts.

The separately [adopted Commission visa guidelines, IP/26/2521](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/api/files/document/print/en/ip_26_2521/IP_26_2521_EN.pdf), concern supporting documents and scrutiny, with protection safeguards. They are not the Council decision activating the combatant visa ban; the release expressly reserves entry into force to the Council. No enacted 22nd package was verified in the Council's [sanctions timeline](https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/policies/sanctions-against-russia/timeline-sanctions-against-russia/) at this review. The [November 16 implementing regulation (EU) 2026/2574](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg_impl/2026/2574/oj/eng) is a different adopted listing action; don't sum those designations with the proposal or treat the July 21st package as new.

**What changes:** Europe proposed tightening specific banking and oil-services channels while the Shenzhen declaration endorsed expanded refined supply without amending sanctions. These are different actors and instruments, and not a legal contradiction. If adopted and enforced, the EU restrictions would raise costs for Russian funding and refinery/shipping services and support Ukraine and allied enforcement objectives; substitution, realized revenue effects, and final scope remain unverified. Track Council adoption, dates, annexes, licenses and actual exclusion/enforcement before scoring. No U.S. sanction, delivered aid or quantified Russian loss is attributed to the proposal.
